# The Seam At Wentworth

If you fly over the southwest corner of New South Wales in the right light, you can see two rivers meeting and not mixing.

The Darling joins the Murray at Wentworth, in country that gets less than 300 mm of rain a year. The Darling comes down from the north carrying clay — fine particles from the cracking floodplains of inland NSW and southern Queensland, where the soil splits open in the dry and seals shut in the wet, releasing clay into every flood that crosses it. Vertosols, soil scientists call them. The clay is what makes the Darling muddy. The clay is what stays in suspension long after gravity should have pulled it out.

The Murray, at this point, has come a long way too — from the Australian Alps, partly snow-fed, partly augmented by the Snowy Scheme's diversions. It carries a different sediment load. It runs cooler. Between the gauges at Euston and Wentworth, irrigation and other losses show up as about 60 cubic metres per second of missing flow, and the Murray still arrives carrying roughly four times the Darling's volume.

The two flows meet. They do not immediately blend.

For some distance downstream — kilometres, by aerial accounts — there is a visible seam in the water. The lighter, muddier thread of the Darling alongside the clearer, greener thread of the Murray. Different turbidities, slightly different densities, slightly different temperatures. The boundary holds because nothing has yet done the work of mixing it: not enough turbulence, not enough channel asymmetry, not enough time to throw the streamlines into each other.

Eventually they do mix. The combined river — now just "the Murray" — flows west through wine country, crosses into South Australia, and several hundred kilometres on reaches the sea at Goolwa.

What's worth knowing about this is not that the seam exists — every confluence has one, briefly. It's that the Darling's clay holds the seam open for so long. The river it joins is bigger, faster, cooler, but the older, slower, muddier flow from inland doesn't dissolve into it on the timescale you'd expect.

Most of the Murray–Darling Basin's water comes from a small slice of its area: the wet southeastern fringe. The Darling drains 57% of the basin and contributes maybe a tenth of the water in a typical year. A vast catchment, mostly dry, producing a thin stream of clay-laden water that, when it finally meets the snow-fed Murray, doesn't dissolve quietly.

You can see the seam from the air. You can probably see it from the bridge at Wentworth on a clear day. It's a small geographic fact about a country with a lot of water-management arguments wrapped around it, and it happens whether or not anyone is watching.
