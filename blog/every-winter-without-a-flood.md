# Every Winter Without A Flood

There's a kind of labor whose proof is the absence of the problem it would have caused.

A bridge that holds. A protocol that doesn't deadlock. A meeting that doesn't happen because the right note got sent the week before. The engineer who, two years ago, chose the constraint that prevents the bug you'll never see. The teacher whose students don't develop the misconception that would otherwise take ten years to unwind. The designer whose interface is "obvious" — meaning: someone spent two months making it obvious.

This labor has a recurring fate. It gets miscredited, sometimes to no one at all — the silence reads as *nothing was needed*. Sometimes to the person who fixed the noisy version of the same problem after it broke — they at least left fingerprints. Almost never to the person whose work was good enough that no fingerprints exist.

The problem isn't ingratitude. It's an information problem. Counterfactuals don't generate evidence. We see what happened, not what was prevented. So when an organization tries to identify who is producing value, the signal is dominated by people whose work leaves marks — including the marks of failures they then fixed. The silent worker has nothing on their dashboard.

The consequences compound.

Promotion goes to firefighters, not fire-preventers, and a whole layer of incentive bends toward making problems visible enough to take credit for solving them. Prevention budgets get cut when nothing is going wrong, that being the point. Every winter without a flood is an argument someone uses to drain the levee budget. People stop doing it. If you've ever been the person who closed off a failure three steps back and watched someone else get the bonus for handling its noisier sibling, you remember.

I want to say *and the solution is*, but I don't have one. You can't reliably surface silent work without changing it — the moment you try to count prevented disasters, people start manufacturing prevented disasters to count. The metric eats itself.

What might help is just the recognition that the bias exists. When you look around at who is producing value, you are seeing a heavily biased sample, weighted toward people whose work leaves marks. The competence that produced the absence of a problem is still competence. The person who saw the failure mode three steps back and quietly closed it off is still doing the work — just not the kind of work the dashboard knows how to display.

If you can, find them. Tell them you noticed. That might be the only correction available.
